Technique 01 · Foundations

The same mic, moved, is a different instrument

Two things decide almost everything about how a mic captures a source: how far it sits from that source, and what angle it's aimed at. Move a mic six inches and the tone changes; rotate it 45° and the room around it changes. Every technique in this chapter is really just a deliberate choice about distance and angle — now in an actual 3D room you can orbit around.

The core trade-off underneath every technique is the ratio of direct sound (arriving straight from the source) to reflected sound (bouncing off the room first). Close in, direct sound dominates — detailed, present, isolated from the room. Pull back, and reflected sound catches up — more natural, more spacious, but less precise.

Technique 02 · Placement

Close Miking

The mic sits inches from the source — anywhere from about two inches to a foot. Direct sound completely dominates, room sound barely registers, and isolation from everything else in the room is close to total. It's the default for tracking sessions where every source needs its own clean channel.

Strengths
  • Maximum detail & presence
  • Near-total isolation from bleed
  • Strong proximity-effect warmth (directional mics)
Trade-offs
  • Loses the room entirely
  • Very sensitive to small position changes
  • Plosives/breath noise up close

Technique 03 · Placement

Spot Miking

A dedicated close mic on one performer or section inside a larger ensemble, blended underneath a set of main/room mics rather than replacing them. The main mics still carry the overall picture — the spot mic just reinforces one part of it that would otherwise get buried.

Used sparingly and mixed low, a spot mic adds clarity without making the ensemble sound like a pile of close-miked instruments. Overused, it collapses the natural balance the main mics were capturing in the first place.

Strengths
  • Reinforces a buried soloist/section
  • Gives the mix engineer a rescue channel
  • Keeps the main mics' overall balance intact
Trade-offs
  • Can sound disconnected from the room if overused
  • Adds a bleed/phase relationship to manage

Technique 04 · Placement

Distant / Room Miking

Pull a mic several feet — sometimes tens of feet — away from the source, and the room stops being background noise and becomes part of the instrument. What you gain is a natural, cohesive picture of the whole source at once; what you lose is up-close detail and control over how much room you're getting.

Strengths
  • Natural, spacious, cohesive image
  • Captures how the source really sounds in the room
  • Great layered under closer mics
Trade-offs
  • Room quality and treatment matter enormously
  • Little isolation from other sources
  • Loses fine transient detail

Technique 05 · Placement

Stereo Miking

Two mics, one stereo image. How they're spaced and angled relative to each other determines everything — get it right and the source translates as a wide, coherent picture that still sums cleanly to mono; get it wrong and instruments smear or partially cancel when the two channels combine.

Keep the 3:1 rule in mind any time two mics can hear the same source: the second mic should be at least three times farther from the first mic than the first mic is from the source, or the two signals start to comb-filter each other.

Spaced Pair: widest, least mono-safe
X-Y: coincident, perfectly mono-safe
ORTF: near-coincident, natural width

Technique 06 · Placement

Multi Miking

Several mics on one source or ensemble at once, each on its own channel, mixed together afterward — a kick mic, a snare mic, an overhead pair, and a room mic all recording the same drum kit simultaneously. It trades simplicity for total control: every element can be balanced, processed, and panned independently at mixdown.

Technique 07 · Characteristics

Proximity Effect

Move a directional mic closer to a source and the low end swells — a bass boost with no EQ involved. It's a side effect of how pressure-gradient capsules work (anything with a rear opening: cardioid, bidirectional, ribbon), and it only happens up close, roughly within a foot or two of the diaphragm.

A true pressure-only capsule — an omnidirectional mic — has no rear opening to create that gradient, so it stays flat no matter how close it gets.
