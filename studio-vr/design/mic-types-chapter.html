Lesson 01 · Foundations

A microphone is a translator, not a recorder

Every microphone does the same basic job: it turns air pressure into voltage. Nothing about that conversion is neutral — the physical part that does the converting (the transducer) has its own weight, its own resistance to moving, its own way of coloring what passes through it. Learn the transducer and you can predict the sound before you ever plug one in.

The next lesson lets you switch between all five transducer families — Dynamic, Condenser (FET), Condenser (Tube), Ribbon, and Contact mics — in one place, then covers how their pickup shape (polar pattern) and specs combine into a choice for a real source.

Why this matters
Picking the right mic is half the battle before a fader ever moves — the wrong transducer on a source can't be fully undone with EQ later, because it never captured the missing detail in the first place.

Lesson 03 · Characteristics

Polar Patterns

A polar pattern is a map of how sensitive a mic is to sound arriving from different directions around it. It's not a hardware add-on — it falls directly out of how the capsule is built, and it determines what a mic captures and, just as importantly, what it rejects.

The mic stays fixed at the center below. Pick a pattern, then click one of the 8 predefined positions to move the sound source there — the lobe shows roughly how much of it that pattern picks up, and the panel plays what it actually sounds like from that spot.

Lesson 04 · Selection

Choosing the right mic

There's no universally "best" microphone — only the best microphone for a specific source, in a specific room, for a specific purpose. Tap a scenario below to see what most engineers reach for first, and why.

Lead Vocal (studio)🎤
Condenser (FET/Tube)Cardioid
A vocal has huge dynamic range and fine detail — breath, sibilance, vibrato — that only a sensitive, low-mass diaphragm captures fully. In a treated room, the condenser's extra handling and room sensitivity stops being a liability.
Live Vocal (stage)🔊
DynamicCardioid
On a loud stage, durability and rejection matter more than a few dB of extra detail — a dynamic shrugs off handling, monitor spill, and high SPL, and its tighter pattern buys more gain before feedback.
Loud Guitar Amp🎸
DynamicRibbon
A cranked amp is loud and harsh up close — a dynamic handles the SPL without strain, while a ribbon's natural top-end roll-off can smooth out fizzy distortion without extra EQ.
Acoustic Guitar🪕
Small-diaphragm CondenserCardioid/Omni
Acoustic instruments live and die on transient detail and even frequency response — exactly what a light, fast condenser diaphragm is built for.
Drum Overheads🥁
Matched Condenser PairCardioid/Omni
Overheads need to capture the whole kit's balance and shimmer accurately across a wide stereo image — a matched condenser pair keeps both channels tonally identical.
Podcast / Voiceover🎧
Dynamic or CondenserCardioid
In an untreated room, a dynamic's tighter pattern and lower room sensitivity often sounds cleaner than a condenser; in a treated booth, a condenser's extra detail wins.
Noisy Environment / Field🏗️
Contact MicTight Cardioid
When ambient noise can't be controlled, sensing vibration through the source itself (contact mic) beats fighting the room through the air with any air mic.
Instrument Body / Resonance📟
Contact Mic
Clamped directly to a soundboard, cajon shell, or body panel, a contact mic captures resonance no air mic pointed at the same spot ever could.
